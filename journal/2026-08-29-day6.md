## What I learned

### Morning: why did we build it that way

We spent the morning looking backward at our Chinook dimensional models, and the question on the screen was not what did you build but why did you build it that way. That reframe did a lot of work on me. Last week I could construct a star schema. This week I can defend one.

The ideas that stuck:

**Grain is a contract.** One row equals one invoice line. Once you declare that, every measure on the table has to be true at that level. I keep thinking of it as the difference between a headline and the fine print, except in data the fine print goes first.

**The BY test.** Revenue by genre. Sales by month. Spending by customer. The words after "by" reveal your dimensions, and the thing being measured is your fact. Facts tell us what happened, dimensions tell us the by. As a writer I appreciate any framework you can carry in a single preposition.

**How data is stored is not always how data is best consumed.** Normalization is for running the business, dimensional models are for understanding it. We did not break the rules from Week 5. We just learned there are two sets of rules for two different jobs.

**Validation is not vibes.** A successful query is not enough. Validate the grain, validate uniqueness, validate the business numbers, and when something is wrong, change the structure, not the truth.

### Afternoon: a commit is a snapshot you can trace back

Then Sir Simonee walked us through Git and GitHub, and something clicked. A pipeline is not finished just because the SQL works. It has to survive change, bugs, new requirements, and new engineers. Working code needs to become managed code.

Version control answers three questions I have wished for in every marketing team I have ever been on: what changed, why did it change, who changed it. No more pipeline_final_FINAL.sql. One file, with history.

We made our first commits from Databricks to GitHub, and I watched my own name appear in a commit log with a timestamp. Change, validate, commit, push. A commit is a snapshot of change you can always trace back. I have been calling my drafts versions for years. Now my versions have receipts.

Sir Hans of Z-Lift Solutions gave the line I will not forget: documentation is engineering infrastructure. Document what others cannot easily infer. Git can say what changed, but only the docs can say why it was designed this way and how to run it. Writing is not the thing you do after engineering. It is part of the engineering.

## Terms I am still learning
- **Grain**: the exact meaning of one row in a table, declared before anything else is built
- **Fact vs dimension**: the fact is the measure, the dimensions are the words after "by"
- **Commit**: a meaningful checkpoint of change, with a message that says why

## What confused me
- Git the tool versus GitHub the host, until the lecture compressed it into one line: Git tracks, GitHub shares
- When a change deserves its own branch versus a direct commit. My team answered this a few days later with a working rule: every edit starts on a task based branch, and one person merges to main
